# F4 over finite fields

*Code: `engine/mpoly/src/f4.rs`, `sigf4.rs`, `order.rs`. Papers: Faugère
1999 (F4); Gebauer and Möller 1988; Giovini, Mora et al. on sugar; Eder
and Faugère's survey of signature algorithms (2017); Roune and Stillman
(2012).*

## The idea in one paragraph

Buchberger reduces one S-polynomial at a time. F4 takes **all pairs of
the lowest degree** at once and turns their reductions into one sparse
matrix:

- one row per S-pair half;
- one row $m g$ for every monomial that a leading monomial divides,
  found by *symbolic preprocessing*.

Row reduction then does every reduction simultaneously, and the rows with
new leading monomials join the basis. The linear algebra (article 1) is
where the time goes. Everything in this article decides which matrix gets
built.

## Representation

A monomial is one `u64`: $n$ fields of `bits` bits, the first variable
most significant. Each field has a top **guard bit**. Then:

- multiplying monomials is adding words;
- divisibility is a borrow test: $a \mid b$ iff
  `((b | guard) - a) & guard == guard`;
- an overflow shows as a set guard bit.

Order keys (`u128`) are computed once per monomial. On overflow the whole
computation restarts with twice the bits; this is rare in degree orders.

## Top to bottom

1. **Generators**: sorted, merged, and echelonized together by increasing
   leading monomial.
2. **Pair queue**: Gebauer–Möller installation for each new element. Pairs
   with coprime leading monomials are dropped, chains are pruned, and
   older pairs that the new element makes redundant are removed. Each pair
   carries its **sugar degree**.
3. **Selection**: every pair of minimal sugar, Faugère's normal strategy.
   Our selection matched msolve's exactly. On katsura-9 degree 7, both
   select 539 pairs and find 56 new elements, which is a useful
   consistency check against an oracle.
4. **Symbolic preprocessing**: a worklist of monomials. For each one that
   some leading monomial divides, add the reducer $m g$ (preferring active
   elements, then the shortest) and push its monomials.
5. **Matrix and elimination**: `spelim`, with $p < 2^{31}$.
6. **Update**: add the new rows, and deactivate elements whose leading
   monomial a new one divides.
7. **Finish**: keep the minimal elements, then reduce all tails in **one
   more matrix** (the tails as rows, no echelon). The result is the
   reduced basis.

## Lex orders: do not run F4 in lex

F4 in lex order with sugar selection blows up. A single sugar degree can
contain reductions whose intermediate polynomials have huge degree in the
last variables.

The fix is homogenization. Add $h$, order by degree, then by the lex
order, with $h$ last; this is a degree order on the homogenized ideal.
Its basis dehomogenizes to a lex Gröbner basis. Reduce that basis one
element at a time, in increasing order (`interreduce_seq`), cancelling as
you go. An interreduction by matrix would collect all monomials of all
tails at once, which in lex can be enormous.

For zero-dimensional ideals, lex now goes through FGLM instead (article
5), which is far better still.

## The signature experiment: correct, and slower

Signature-based algorithms (F5 and its matrix forms) avoid reductions to
zero entirely. Most F4 rows reduce to zero, so this looked like the next
win. `sigf4.rs` implements the matrix version from Eder and Faugère's
survey:

- signatures $u e_i$, ordered by degree, then index, then monomial;
- one row per surviving signature: the multiple of its **canonical
  rewriter**, the last element whose signature divides it;
- signatures divisible by a Koszul syzygy or by an earlier reduction to
  zero are dropped;
- rows reduced in increasing signature, each only by earlier rows.

It is correct (288 random ideals against Singular) and almost free of
zero reductions. It is also slower: cyclic-7 mod $p$ took 1.23 s against
F4's 0.19 s, and homogeneous katsura-8 took 0.10 s against 0.07 s. The
reasons:

- **No interreduction between degrees** (F5C does this). Elements stay
  long, so matrices are wider.
- **No cheap pair pruning.** F4's product and chain criteria remove most
  pairs for free. Signatures remove them only after their rows are
  formed.
- **Higher degrees.** Homogeneous katsura-9 reaches degree 17 against
  F4's 12, and runs past the exponent packing.
- **Two subtle bugs**, each of which made a wrong basis look plausible:
  - a row reduced after another row *with the same signature* is not a
    regular reduction, and recording its vanishing as a syzygy is wrong;
  - homogenizing for deglex is not valid, because deglex on $(x, h)$ does
    not restrict to deglex on $x$. Degrevlex with $h$ last does restrict.

It stays behind `SB_SIGF4` for experiments. Reductions to zero turned out
to be cheap in F4: they cost a cascade or a dense update each, and the
basis stays interreduced. Avoiding them does not pay unless everything
else is done as well as F5C or GVW do it.

## Where F4 still loses

Cyclic-8 mod $p$ is the one benchmark where Magma 2.18 (2.2 s) and msolve
(1.9 s) are 2× faster than us (4.8 s). The likely costs are:

- symbolic preprocessing and matrix building, which are sequential,
  hash-based and do a linear reducer search per monomial;
- reducer choice ("Simplify" in Faugère's paper reuses already-reduced
  multiples, which gives sparser rows).

That is the next F4 project.

## What would have saved time

- **Compare per-step statistics with an oracle early.** msolve's `-v 2`
  prints the pairs and matrix sizes per step. Matching them exactly
  proved our selection right, and pointed at the linear algebra rather
  than the strategy.
- **Profile phases, not just totals.** An `SB_F4_DEBUG` split of
  preprocessing, matrix building and reduction should exist from the
  first commit.
- **Be careful when a Gröbner basis "looks fine".** Random ideals compared
  with Singular, in several orders and fields, caught every bug above;
  benchmark families did not.
