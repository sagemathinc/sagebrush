# L^2: 1.7x FLINT on the benchmark corpus

*Papers: Nguyen and Stehlé,
"An LLL algorithm with quadratic complexity" (2009); Stehlé's surveys
of floating-point LLL.*

## The idea in one paragraph

The exact LLL (article 1) pays for exactness with numbers about $d$
times longer than the input. L^2 keeps the basis exact but computes the
Gram–Schmidt data $r_{ij}$ and $\mu_{ij}$ in floating point, from the
exact Gram matrix $G = B B^T$. Nguyen and Stehlé showed that about
$1.6\,d$ bits of precision are enough. Their *lazy size reduction*
recomputes row $k$'s floating-point data, subtracts rounded multiples of
the earlier rows, and repeats until $|\mu_{kj}| \le \eta'$ (a little above
$0.51$, to absorb rounding). In practice, far fewer bits than $1.6\,d$
usually suffice, so precision becomes something to choose and escalate,
not a fixed cost.

## Top to bottom

1. **Rows.** A basis row is one of three things, and rows move between
   them:
   - `S`: machine integers, while every entry is below $2^{62}$;
   - `W`: fixed-width limbs, so a row operation $b_k \leftarrow b_k - X b_j$
     allocates nothing;
   - `B`: big integers.

   A big-integer entry operation costs about 14 limb operations at any
   size up to about 1000 bits (measured during development: about 32 ns
   against 2.3 ns per limb). So a row goes to limbs when $n \cdot w \le 14 \cdot \text{nnz}$:
   $n$ entries of $w$ limbs against its nonzero big integers.
2. **A ladder of rungs.** Each rung starts from the basis the previous
   one reached, and a result is returned only once proved (article 3):

   | rung | arithmetic | Gram matrix |
   |---|---|---|
   | 0 | plain `f64`, scaled by per-row exponents | approximate, from 63-bit row copies (exact when the dot product cancels below $2^{-30}$) |
   | 1 | `f64` with an `i64` exponent | the same |
   | 2 | plain double-double, scaled by row exponents | from 106-bit row copies (exact below $2^{-60}$) |
   | 3 | double-double with an exponent | the same |
   | 4 | double-double | the exact Gram matrix |

3. **Lazy size reduction and Lovász**, as in the paper, with $\eta'$ and
   $\delta'$ slightly looser than $0.51$ and $0.99$. If the largest
   $|\mu|$ stops shrinking over a few rounds, precision has run out, and
   the next rung takes over.
4. **The fallback.** If the last rung cannot finish, or its result cannot
   be proved, the exact LLL starts over from the input.

## From 15x slower to 1.7x, in two hours

The first version that certified on every case was 11 to 17 times
slower than FLINT. Each step below was measured on the whole corpus, and
every output was still certified (article 1). These are development
measurements, taken on a busy shared machine as the work went on, so
they show the trend, not final numbers:

| step | ours / FLINT |
|---|---|
| first working version (exact Gram matrix, `f64` with exponent) | 11-17x |
| powers of two instead of general exponents; no row clones | 8-12x |
| an approximate Gram matrix from floating-point row copies, exact only on cancellation | 3-9x |
| rung 0: plain doubles scaled by row exponents | 2-6x |
| machine-word rows (`i64`, checked `i128` products, promote and demote) | 0.7-2.8x, most within 2x |
| the final ladder and proof checks | geometric mean 1.68x over 77 cases |

The development profile after the first version said where to look: 65 to 80% of the
time went to big-integer row and Gram updates, not to floating point. Most
of the speed came from not touching big integers when the numbers fit in
a word, and from not recomputing exact dot products that floating point
gets right.

## What did not work

- **Choosing the rung up front.** The first design guessed the precision
  from the input and stayed there. Development traces showed rung 0 doing real work
  even on the hardest cases: 1.39 million swaps on a 200-dimensional
  Goldstein–Mayer lattice in 69 seconds. The exact-Gram rung managed
  only about 650 swaps per second. Starting cheap and escalating beats
  guessing.
- **The exact Gram matrix in every rung.** It is what the paper
  suggests, and it is correct. But updating big integers on every swap
  cost more than the floating point. Approximate Gram matrices, exact
  only where cancellation makes them wrong, kept the speed. The proof at
  the end (article 3) is what makes the shortcut safe.

## Where it still loses

- **Large entries at $d = 200$.** For knapsack lattices with 1000-bit
  entries, rung 0's cost per swap is higher than FLINT's, 2.8x
  overall. Compressing the entries first is the next step (article 6).
- **The smallest cases.** Below about 10 ms, fixed overheads dominate,
  and the ratios are noisy.
- **Ajtai lattices.** These need the most precision, and spend most of
  their time in the double-double rungs.

## What would have saved time

- **Profile the first working version, not the design.** The prediction
  was that floating point would dominate. It did not: big integers did.
- **An honest corpus from the start.** Five families (knapsack,
  Goldstein–Mayer, NTRU, uniform, Ajtai) at $d$ from 10 to 200, frozen
  with checksums before the first line of L^2. Each change was judged on
  all 78 cases, so a speed-up that helped one family and hurt another
  showed at once.
- **A certificate before speed** (article 1). Every step in the table
  above changed how numbers are computed. None was accepted without all
  outputs certifying.
